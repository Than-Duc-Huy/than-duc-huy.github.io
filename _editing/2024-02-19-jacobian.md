## Spatial Representation

### Pose/Configuration

- To describe a pose (or configuration) in space of a coordinate frame, we need:
  - Translation
  - Orientation

| Description                | Mathematical Representation                                                                                                      | Dimension                      |
| -------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Position/Translation       | $\mathbf {p} = [x,y,z]$                                                                                                          | $\mathbb R^3$                  |
| Rotation Matrix            | $\mathbf {R} = \begin{bmatrix} r_{xx} & r_{yx} & r_{zx} \\\ r_{xy} & r_{yy} & r_{zy} \\\ r_{xz} & r_{yz} & r_{zz} \end{bmatrix}$ | $\mathbb R^{3\times 3}, SO(3)$ |
| Homogeneous Transformation | $\mathbf T = \begin{bmatrix}\bf{R} & \bf{p} \\\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$                                                              | $\mathbb R^{4\times 4}, SE(3)$ |

## Velocity

- In terms of intuition, similar to pose:

  - Translational component ($x,y,z$) is easy to understand as linear velocity along the respective axes $\dot x, \dot y, \dot z$
  - Angular, Rotation component is hard to understand
    - We cannot differentiate the rotation matrix $\mathbb R^{3\times 3}$
    - How to rotate "along" 3 different axes?
    - Introducing quaternion can also be troublesome

- **Hence** we use the idea of **Twist**, which is built from the idea of a Screw

### Screw

-

Every transformation (translate and rotate) can be parameterized (written) as a rotation around an axis $\omega$

- Previously, we construct a transformation from a combination Rotation and Translation: $Tran \times Rot$, which can mean
  - Global Rotation then Global Translation: (post-multipy, from right to left)
  - Local Translation first then Local Rotation (pre-multiply, from left to right)
- But constructing the homogeneous transformation like this appear out of nowhere...

  - How do we know that the rotation matrix get this way?

- Another representation is to think of the joints of the robot. Since it is positioned in a certain direction, it will have the tendency to rotate around its axis. When we define the rotation axis $\omega = \begin{bmatrix} \omega_x \\\ \omega_y \\\ \omega_z \end{bmatrix}$

- Since it has the tendency to rotate around this axis, we can also define $\omega$ as the angular velocity of that joint around that axis. And angle of the joint, $\theta$, multiply with the axis of rotation to give the exact angular position

> Hence, instead of defining the rotation as a matrix, then struggle to find the velocity, we define the _rotation in terms of the angular velocity_ first, then derive the angular position
